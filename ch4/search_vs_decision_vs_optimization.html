29  Decision, search and optimization problems

% noend hides “end for”; drop it to keep them

%

We use a CNF formula F to illustrate three possible different types of natural questions we can ask about computational problems: decision, search, and optimization. Very likely you have already seen the definition of formulas in Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF), if not look at the beginning of Chapter 33.

Click on the panels below to expand on each of them.

  • Decision
  • Search
  • Optimization

Is F satisfiable? The answer is a single bit, yes or no.

A decision problem is a language L \subseteq \Sigma^*. Given w \in \Sigma^*, we must decide whether w \in L.

Find a satisfying assignment of F, or report that there is none. The answer is an object, here an assignment.

A search problem is given by a relation R \subseteq \Sigma^* \times \Sigma^*. Given w, we must find some y with (w, y) \in R, or report that no such y exists. We call such a y a solution for w. For \mathtt{SAT}, the pair (\langle F \rangle, \alpha) is in R when \alpha is an assignment that satisfies F (see Chapter 33).

Find an assignment that satisfies as many clauses of F as possible. The answer is an object that is best with respect to some cost function.

An optimization problem is given by a set of feasible solutions for each input w and a cost function c(w, y). Given w, we must find a feasible solution y with the largest (or smallest) possible value of c(w, y). For \mathsf{MAX}\text{-}\mathtt{SAT}, every assignment is feasible, and c(\langle F \rangle, a) is the number of clauses of F that a satisfies.

Every optimization problem has a decision version, obtained by adding a threshold k to the input. For example, \mathsf{MAX}\text{-}\mathtt{SAT} becomes: given F and k, is there an assignment that satisfies at least k clauses of F?

Example 29.1 Here are a few more examples:

Decision Search Optimization
Does the graph G have a clique of size k? Find a clique of size k in G. Find a largest clique in G.
Is there a tour of length at most k that visits every city? Find such a tour. Find a shortest tour (TSP).
Is n composite? Find a nontrivial factor of n. Find the smallest prime factor of n.

29.1 Why we focus on decision problems

In complexity theory we almost always work with decision problems. There are several reasons for this.

They are languages. A decision problem is a language, and a TM that solves it simply accepts or rejects, exactly as in the computability part of the course. So we can keep using the same model, and complexity classes such as \mathbf{P} and \mathbf{NP} are simply sets of languages. There is no need to say what it means for a TM to “output” an assignment or a tour, or to compare the quality of outputs.

Reductions are simpler. A many-one reduction L_1 \leqslant_p L_2 only has to map yes-instances to yes-instances and no-instances to no-instances. Reductions between search or optimization problems also have to translate solutions back, which makes them more cumbersome to define and to use.

Hardness transfers upwards. A decision problem is never harder than its search or optimization versions. If we can find a satisfying assignment, we can certainly tell whether one exists. If we can find a largest clique, we can tell whether there is a clique of size k. So if the decision version is hard, the other versions are hard too. Since lower bounds are what we are after (for example, showing that \mathtt{SAT}\notin \mathbf{P}), nothing is lost by proving them for the decision version.

Usually, nothing is lost at all. For many natural problems, the converse also holds: a polynomial-time algorithm for the decision version gives a polynomial-time algorithm for the search and optimization versions.

  • From optimization to decision: to compute the optimal value, run binary search over the threshold k. The optimum is at most 2^{p(|w|)} for some polynomial p, so only \mathcal{O}(p(|w|)) calls to the decision algorithm are needed.
  • From search to decision: many problems in \mathbf{NP} are self-reducible, meaning that we can build a solution one piece at a time by asking decision questions. Exercise 33.4 shows how to do this for \mathtt{SAT}.

So, for problems like \mathtt{SAT}, \mathtt{CLIQUE} or \mathtt{TSP}, the three versions are either all solvable in polynomial time or none of them is. The decision version captures the difficulty of the problem.

WarningCaution

Search can be harder than decision when the decision question doesn’t really ask about the solutions. A standard example is factoring. Deciding whether n is composite is in \mathbf{P} (by the AKS primality test, 2002), but no polynomial-time algorithm is known for finding a nontrivial factor of n on a classical computer. Here the decision algorithm tells us that a factor exists without giving us any information about what it is. The security of RSA cryptosystem depends on this gap.

On a quantum computer, the situation is different: Shor’s algorithm (1994) factors integers in polynomial time. Quantum computers large enough to break RSA in practice do not exist yet, but this has motivated post-quantum cryptography, which builds cryptosystems on problems that are believed to be hard even for quantum computers, such as problems on lattices. In 2024, NIST standardized the first such schemes (ML-KEM, ML-DSA and SLH-DSA), and they are now being deployed to replace RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography.
