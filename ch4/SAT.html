33  \mathtt{SAT} is \mathbf{NP}-complete

% noend hides “end for”; drop it to keep them

In this chapter we introduce (recall?) what \mathtt{SAT} is, we prove that it is \mathbf{NP}-complete, and we show some variations of it that are also \mathbf{NP}-complete and others that are in \mathbf{P}.

33.1 Propositional formulas

We consider Boolean (i.e. 0/1-valued) variables x_1, x_2, x_3, \dots. A literal is a variable x_i or its negation \neg x_i. A clause is a disjunction (\lor) of one or more literals. A formula in conjunctive normal form (CNF) is a conjunction (\land) of one or more clauses: F = C_1 \wedge C_2 \wedge \dots \wedge C_m, \qquad C_j = (\ell_{j,1} \vee \ell_{j,2} \vee \dots \vee \ell_{j,k_j}).

To give F as input to a Turing machine, we write it as a word over the finite alphabet \Sigma_{\mathtt{SAT}} = \{\, \mathtt{x},\ \mathtt{0},\ \mathtt{1},\ \neg,\ \vee,\ \wedge,\ (,\ )\,\}. There are infinitely many variables, so a variable can’t be a single symbol. We write its index in binary instead. The encoding \langle\cdot\rangle is defined as follows:

  • \langle x_i \rangle = \mathtt{x}\,\mathrm{bin}(i), where \mathrm{bin}(i) \in \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{1}}\{\textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{0}},\textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{1}}\}^* is i \geqslant 1 in binary;
  • \langle \neg x_i \rangle = \neg\,\langle x_i \rangle;
  • \langle C \rangle = (\,\langle \ell_1 \rangle \vee \langle \ell_2 \rangle \vee \dots \vee \langle \ell_k \rangle\,) for a clause C = \ell_1 \vee \dots \vee \ell_k;
  • \langle F \rangle = \langle C_1 \rangle \wedge \langle C_2 \rangle \wedge \dots \wedge \langle C_m \rangle for a formula F = C_1 \wedge \dots \wedge C_m.

Example 33.1 The formula F = (x_1 \vee \neg x_2) \wedge (x_2 \vee x_3 \vee \neg x_1) is encoded as the word \langle F \rangle = (\mathtt{x1} \vee \neg\mathtt{x10}) \wedge (\mathtt{x10} \vee \mathtt{x11} \vee \neg\mathtt{x1}).

An assignment is a map \alpha\colon \{x_1,\dots,x_n\} \to \{0,1\}.1 It satisfies F if every clause contains at least one true literal: either some x_i with \alpha(x_i) = 1 or some \neg x_i with \alpha(x_i) = 0. The formula F is satisfiable if some assignment satisfies it.

Example 33.2 (Satisfiable or not?) For each of the following CNF formulas, decide whether it is satisfiable. If it is, give a satisfying assignment. If it is not, explain why no assignment satisfies it.

  1. F_1 = (x_1 \lor \lnot x_2 \lor x_3) \land (\lnot x_1 \lor x_2) \land (\lnot x_2 \lor \lnot x_3) \land (\lnot x_1 \lor x_3)
  2. F_2 = (x_1 \lor x_2) \land (\lnot x_1 \lor x_3) \land (\lnot x_2 \lor x_3) \land (\lnot x_3 \lor x_1) \land (\lnot x_3 \lor \lnot x_1)
TipHint

You can always check all 2^3 = 8 assignments, but it is quicker to reason with the short clauses. Read a clause (\lnot x \lor y) as the implication x \to y, and see what follows from setting a variable to 1 or to 0.

NoteSolution

  1. Satisfiable. Suppose x_1 = 1. Then the clauses (\lnot x_1 \lor x_2) and (\lnot x_1 \lor x_3) force x_2 = x_3 = 1, but then (\lnot x_2 \lor \lnot x_3) is false. So x_1 = 0. The first clause then becomes (\lnot x_2 \lor x_3), and setting x_2 = 0 satisfies it. So x_1 = x_2 = x_3 = 0 satisfies F_1. The assignment x_1 = x_2 = 0, x_3 = 1 is the only other one that does.

  2. Unsatisfiable. The clauses (\lnot x_3 \lor x_1) and (\lnot x_3 \lor \lnot x_1) say that x_3 \to x_1 and x_3 \to \lnot x_1, so every satisfying assignment must have x_3 = 0. Then (\lnot x_1 \lor x_3) forces x_1 = 0, and (\lnot x_2 \lor x_3) forces x_2 = 0. But then (x_1 \lor x_2) is false. Hence no assignment satisfies F_2.

Exercise 33.1 (\mathrm{PHP}^m_n) In this exercise we construct a propositional formula encoding the pigenhole principle (see Section 2.2.1). For n\in \mathbb{N}, let [n]=\{1,\dots,n\}. For each i\in [m] and j\in [n], let p_{i,j} be a Boolean variable. The intended meaning of p_{i,j} is that p_{i,j}=1 if and only if the pigeon i flies to hole j. Consider the following CNF formula: \mathrm{PHP}^m_n=\bigwedge_{i\in [m]}(p_{i,1}\lor \cdots\lor p_{i,n})\ \land\ \bigwedge_{j\in [n]}\bigwedge_{\substack{i,i'\in [m]\\ i\neq i'}} (\lnot p_{i,j}\lor \lnot p_{i',j})\ . \tag{33.1}

  1. Show that an assignment that satisfies p_{i,1}\lor \cdots\lor p_{i,n} also satisfies p_{i,1}+ \cdots+ p_{i,n}\geqslant 1 (and viceversa);
  2. Show that an assignment that satisfies \bigwedge_{\substack{i,i'\in [m]\\ j\neq j'}} (\lnot p_{i,j}\lor \lnot p_{i',j}) also satisfies p_{1,j}+ \cdots+ p_{m,j}\leqslant 1 (and viceversa);
  3. Show that \langle\mathrm{PHP}^m_n\rangle\in \mathtt{SAT} if and only if m\leqslant n.

33.2 \mathtt{SAT}

In this section we recall more precisely what \mathtt{SAT} is and we prove that it is \mathbf{NP}-complete. This language will be our first \mathbf{NP}-complete language and via reductions will allow to show that many more languages are \mathbf{NP}-complete.

Definition 33.1 (\mathtt{SAT}) \mathtt{SAT}= \{\, \langle F \rangle \in \Sigma_{\mathtt{SAT}}^* :F \text{ is a CNF formula and } F \text{ is satisfiable} \,\}. A word that is not the encoding of any CNF formula is not in \mathtt{SAT}.

We have the following observations, which are left as an exercise about how some details of the definition of \mathtt{SAT} do not actually matter.

Exercise 33.2 (The encoding does not matter much)  

  1. The encoding \langle \cdot \rangle is short. Suppose F has n variables, m clauses and L literal occurrences in total. Show that each literal takes at most 2 + \lfloor \log_2 n \rfloor + 1 symbols, and there are fewer than L + 3m separator symbols. So |\langle F\rangle| = \mathcal{O}(L \log n).
  2. Checking the syntax is easy. Show that the set of words that encode some CNF formula is a regular language. A TM can therefore reject badly formed inputs in linear time. (Hence difficulty of \mathtt{SAT} lies entirely in deciding satisfiability.)
  3. The choice of encoding \langle \cdot \rangle does not matter. Any reasonable encoding gives a word whose length is polynomially related to the length of the one we used. For example, give an encoding writing indices in unary, and total length \mathcal{O}(Ln), or list the variable count and clause count first. (A TM can translate between these encodings in polynomial time, so membership of \mathtt{SAT} in \mathbf{P} or \mathbf{NP}, and its \mathbf{NP}-completeness, do not depend on the encoding we chose.)

A well-known property is that \mathtt{SAT}\in \mathbf{EXP}.

Exercise 33.3 (\mathtt{SAT} in exponential time) Let F be a CNF formula, let N = |\langle F\rangle| be the length of its encoding, and let n be the number of distinct variables that appear in F.

  1. Describe a multitape Turing machine that decides \mathtt{SAT} in time \mathcal{O}(2^n \cdot N^2). Give a high-level description of its tapes and phases, and estimate the asymptotic cost of each phase.

    TipHint

    There are 2^n assignments to the variables of F. How long does it take to check whether a single assignment satisfies F? Keep the current assignment on a separate tape and treat it as a binary counter.

  2. Show that n \leqslant N/3.

  3. Conclude that \mathtt{SAT}\in \mathbf{TIME}(2^N), and therefore \mathtt{SAT}\in \mathbf{EXP}.

We do not know whether \mathtt{SAT}\in \mathbf{P}. But we know that \mathtt{SAT} is \mathbf{NP}-complete.

Theorem 33.1 (Cook-Levin) \mathtt{SAT} is \mathbf{NP}-complete.

NoteProof (sketch)

We have to show that \mathtt{SAT}\in \mathbf{NP}, and that L \leqslant_p \mathtt{SAT} for every L \in \mathbf{NP}.

\mathtt{SAT}\in \mathbf{NP}. The certificate for \langle F \rangle is an assignment \alpha of its variables. Its length is at most |\langle F \rangle|, and a verifier can check in polynomial time that a satisfies every clause of F.

\mathtt{SAT} is \mathbf{NP}-hard. Let L \in \mathbf{NP}. Then there is a single-tape nondeterministic TM M = (Q, \Sigma, \Gamma, \delta, q_0, q_{\text{acc}}, q_{\text{rej}}) that decides L in time p(n), for some polynomial p. We modify M so that it never stops: once it is in q_{\text{acc}} or q_{\text{rej}}, it stays in that state, does not move its head and does not change the tape. Then every computation of M can be extended to one that runs for exactly p(n) steps.

Let w = w_1 \cdots w_n be an input and let N = p(n). We construct, in polynomial time, a CNF formula F_w such that w \in L \iff F_w \text{ is satisfiable}.

The tableau. We write a configuration of M as a word over \Gamma \cup Q: the tape contents, with the state written just before the cell under the head. In N steps, M visits at most N+1 cells, so each configuration fits in a word of length N+2. A tableau for w is an (N+1) \times (N+4) table whose rows are configurations, padded with blanks \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{\sqcup}} and with a symbol \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{\#}} at each end:

  • row 0 is the start configuration \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{\#}}\, q_0\, w_1 \cdots w_n \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{\sqcup}} \cdots \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{\sqcup \#}};
  • each row i+1 follows from row i by one step of M.

The tableau is accepting if some row contains q_{\text{acc}}. So w \in L if and only if there is an accepting tableau for w.

The variables. Let C = Q \cup \Gamma \cup \{\textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{\#}}\}. For each cell (i,j) and each symbol s \in C, we introduce a variable x_{i,j,s}, meaning that cell (i,j) contains s. We define F_w = F_{\text{cell}} \land F_{\text{start}} \land F_{\text{accept}} \land F_{\text{move}}, where:

  • F_{\text{cell}} says that every cell contains exactly one symbol: F_{\text{cell}} = \bigwedge_{i,j} \Big[ \Big( \bigvee_{s \in C} x_{i,j,s} \Big) \land \bigwedge_{s \neq t} (\lnot x_{i,j,s} \lor \lnot x_{i,j,t}) \Big].
  • F_{\text{start}} says that row 0 is the start configuration. It is a conjunction of single-literal clauses: x_{0,0,\#} \land x_{0,1,q_0} \land x_{0,2,w_1} \land \cdots \land x_{0,N+3,\#}.
  • F_{\text{accept}} says that q_{\text{acc}} appears somewhere. It is a single clause: \bigvee_{i,j} x_{i,j,q_{\text{acc}}}.
  • F_{\text{move}} says that each row follows from the previous one by a step of M. This is the key part, explained next.

Windows. A window is a 2 \times 3 block of cells, with three cells (i, j-1), (i,j), (i,j+1) in row i and the three cells below them in row i+1. The head moves at most one cell per step, so the contents of a cell in row i+1 depend only on the three cells above it. We call a window legal if it is consistent with \delta. For example, if (q', b, R) \in \delta(q, a), then the window with top row x\; q\; a and bottom row x\; b\; q' is legal. The set W \subseteq C^6 of legal windows depends only on M, not on w.

One can then show the following:

If row 0 is the start configuration and every window of the tableau is legal, then every row i+1 is a configuration that follows from row i by one step of M.

So it is enough for F_{\text{move}} to say that every window is legal. We do this by forbidding each illegal window: F_{\text{move}} = \bigwedge_{i,j}\ \bigwedge_{(a_1,\dots,a_6) \notin W} \big( \lnot x_{i,j-1,a_1} \lor \lnot x_{i,j,a_2} \lor \lnot x_{i,j+1,a_3} \lor \lnot x_{i+1,j-1,a_4} \lor \lnot x_{i+1,j,a_5} \lor \lnot x_{i+1,j+1,a_6} \big).

Correctness. If w \in L, then M has an accepting computation on w. Extending it to N steps gives an accepting tableau, and setting x_{i,j,s} = 1 exactly when cell (i,j) contains s satisfies F_w. Conversely, suppose an assignment satisfies F_w. By F_{\text{cell}}, it describes a table with one symbol in each cell. By F_{\text{start}} and F_{\text{move}}, together with the claim above, the rows of the table form a computation of M on w. By F_{\text{accept}}, this computation reaches q_{\text{acc}}. Hence w \in L.

Size and running time. The tableau has (N+1)(N+4) = O(p(n)^2) cells. Each part of F_w contributes a number of clauses per cell that depends only on |C| and |W|, that is, only on M. With indices written in binary, |\langle F_w \rangle| = O(p(n)^2 \log p(n)). The formula can be written out with a few nested loops over i, j and the symbols, so the map w \mapsto \langle F_w \rangle is computable in polynomial time. Therefore L \leqslant_p \mathtt{SAT}.

Exercise 33.4 (Search to decision for \mathtt{SAT}) Assume that \mathtt{SAT}\in \mathbf{P}. Show that there is a polynomial-time algorithm that, given a CNF formula F, either outputs a satisfying assignment of F or reports that F is unsatisfiable.

TipHint

Let x_1, \dots, x_n be the variables of F, and write F|_{x_1 = b} for the formula obtained by setting x_1 to b and simplifying. That is, delete the clauses that become true and delete the literals that become false. Then F is satisfiable if and only if F|_{x_1 = 0} or F|_{x_1 = 1} is satisfiable. Fix the variables one at a time, using the algorithm for \mathtt{SAT} to choose a value for each one. How many times do you call that algorithm? Be careful with clauses that become empty.

Exercise 33.5 (Optimization to decision for \mathsf{MAX}\text{-}\mathtt{SAT}) Let \mathsf{MAX}\text{-}\mathtt{SAT}= \{\, \langle F, k \rangle : \text{some assignment satisfies at least } k \text{ clauses of } F \,\}. Assume that \mathsf{MAX}\text{-}\mathtt{SAT}\in \mathbf{P}. Show that there is a polynomial-time algorithm that, given F, outputs an assignment satisfying the largest possible number of clauses of F.

TipHint

First compute the optimal number of satisfied clauses k^*. Note that 0 \leqslant k^* \leqslant m, where m is the number of clauses. Then fix the variables one at a time, as in Exercise 33.4, keeping the value of each variable only if k^* clauses can still be satisfied. This time, don’t delete the clauses that become true; keep track of how many there are.

33.3 Variants of \mathtt{SAT} \mathbf{NP}-complete

In this section we collect some (somewhat guided) exercises about variations of \mathtt{SAT} which remain \mathbf{NP}-complete.

Exercise 33.6 (k\text{-}\mathtt{SAT}, k\geqslant 3) A CNF formula F is a k-CNF if each clause of F has at most k literals. The language k\text{-}\mathtt{SAT} is k\text{-}\mathtt{SAT}= \{\, \langle F \rangle \in \Sigma_{\mathtt{SAT}}^* :F \text{ is a $k$-CNF formula and } F \text{ is satisfiable} \,\}. Show that k\text{-}\mathtt{SAT} is \mathbf{NP}-complete for every k\geqslant 3.

TipHint

Prove that 3\text{-}\mathtt{SAT}\in \mathbf{NP} and construct a polynomial-time reduction showing that \mathtt{SAT}\leqslant_p 3\text{-}\mathtt{SAT}. For the reduction, let C = \ell_1 \lor \ell_2 \lor \cdots \lor \ell_k be a clause with k \geqslant 4 literals, and let \tilde C = (\ell_1 \lor \ell_2 \lor y_{C,2}) \land (\lnot y_{C,2} \lor \ell_3 \lor y_{C,3}) \land \cdots \land (\lnot y_{C,k-2} \lor \ell_{k-1} \lor \ell_k), where the y_{C,i} are new variables. You can use that an assignment to the variables of C satisfies C if and only if it can be extended to the variables y_{C,i} so that it satisfies \tilde C.

Exercise 33.7 (\mathsf{NAE}\text{-}3\text{-}\mathtt{SAT}) A 3-CNF formula is NAE-satisfiable (not-all-equal satisfiable) if there is an assignment under which every clause has at least one true literal and at least one false literal. Let \mathsf{NAE}\text{-}3\text{-}\mathtt{SAT}= \{\, \langle F \rangle : F \text{ is a } 3\text{-CNF formula that is NAE-satisfiable} \,\}. Show that \mathsf{NAE}\text{-}3\text{-}\mathtt{SAT} is \mathbf{NP}-complete.

TipHint

Prove that \mathsf{NAE}\text{-}3\text{-}\mathtt{SAT}\in \mathbf{NP} and construct a polynomial-time reduction showing that 3\text{-}\mathtt{SAT}\leqslant_p \mathsf{NAE}\text{-}3\text{-}\mathtt{SAT}.

First show that if an assignment a NAE-satisfies F, then so does its complement \bar a, defined by \bar a(x) = 1 - a(x) for every variable x.

For the reduction, take one new variable z shared by the whole formula. For each clause C = \ell_1 \lor \ell_2 \lor \ell_3, take a new variable y_C and let \tilde C = (\ell_1 \lor \ell_2 \lor y_C) \land (\lnot y_C \lor \ell_3 \lor z). You can use that an assignment with z = 0 satisfies C if and only if it can be extended to y_C so that it NAE-satisfies \tilde C. Use the complement property above to explain why you may assume z = 0.

Exercise 33.8 (\mathsf{1\text{-}in\text{-}3\text{-}\mathtt{SAT}}) A 3-CNF formula is 1-in-3-satisfiable if there is an assignment under which every clause has exactly one true literal. Let \mathsf{1\text{-}in\text{-}3\text{-}\mathtt{SAT}} = \{\, \langle \varphi \rangle : \varphi \text{ is a } 3\text{-CNF formula that is 1-in-3-satisfiable} \,\}. Show that \mathsf{1\text{-}in\text{-}3\text{-}\mathtt{SAT}} is \mathbf{NP}-complete.

TipHint

Prove that \mathsf{1\text{-}in\text{-}3\text{-}SAT} \in \mathbf{NP} and construct a polynomial-time reduction showing that 3\text{-}\mathtt{SAT}\leqslant_p \mathsf{1\text{-}in\text{-}3\text{-}SAT}. For the reduction, take four new variables a_C, b_C, c_C, d_C for each clause C = \ell_1 \lor \ell_2 \lor \ell_3, and let \tilde C = (\lnot \ell_1 \lor a_C \lor b_C) \land (b_C \lor \ell_2 \lor c_C) \land (c_C \lor d_C \lor \lnot \ell_3). You can use that an assignment satisfies C if and only if it can be extended to a_C, b_C, c_C, d_C so that every clause of \tilde C has exactly one true literal.

33.4 Variants of \mathtt{SAT} in \mathbf{P}

In this section we collect some (somewhat guided) exercises about variations of \mathtt{SAT} which do not remain \mathbf{NP}-complete, and actually are in \mathbf{P}.

Exercise 33.9 (2\mathtt{SAT}) Given a 2-CNF formula F, its implication graph G_F has one vertex for each literal x and \lnot x. Each clause (\ell_1 \lor \ell_2) adds the edges \lnot \ell_1 \to \ell_2 and \lnot \ell_2 \to \ell_1. A clause (\ell) with a single literal is treated as (\ell \lor \ell).

  1. Show that F is unsatisfiable if and only if there is a variable x such that G_F has a path from x to \lnot x and a path from \lnot x to x.
  2. Show that 2\mathtt{SAT}\in \mathbf{P}.
TipHint

For a., every edge \ell \to \ell' of G_F means that in every satisfying assignment, if \ell is true then so is \ell'. This also holds along paths, which gives one direction. For the other direction, build an assignment step by step. Pick a literal \ell that has no value yet and such that there is no path from \ell to \lnot \ell. Set \ell and every literal reachable from \ell to true, and repeat. Show that this never makes both a literal and its negation true, and that every clause ends up satisfied.

For b., use that it can be decided in polynomial time whether a graph has a path between two given vertices (for example, by breadth-first search).

Exercise 33.10 (\mathsf{HORN}\text{-}\mathtt{SAT}) A Horn clause is a clause with at most one positive literal, such as (\lnot x_1 \lor \lnot x_2 \lor x_3), (\lnot x_1 \lor \lnot x_4) or (x_2). A Horn formula is a CNF formula all of whose clauses are Horn clauses. Let \mathsf{HORN}\text{-}\mathtt{SAT}= \{\, \langle F \rangle : F \text{ is a satisfiable Horn formula} \,\}. Show that \mathsf{HORN}\text{-}\mathtt{SAT}\in \mathbf{P}.

TipHint

A Horn clause (\lnot x_1 \lor \cdots \lor \lnot x_k \lor y) is equivalent to the implication x_1 \land \cdots \land x_k \to y. Consider the following algorithm. Start with all variables set to 0. While some clause is false, do the following:

  • if the false clause has a positive literal y, set y to 1;
  • otherwise, reject.

If no clause is false, accept. Show that every variable the algorithm sets to 1 is equal to 1 in every satisfying assignment of F, and use this to prove that the algorithm is correct. How many times can the loop run?

Exercise 33.11 (\mathsf{XOR}\text{-}\mathtt{SAT}) An XOR clause is an expression \ell_1 \oplus \ell_2 \oplus \cdots \oplus \ell_k, where the \ell_i are literals and \oplus is exclusive or. It is true if and only if an odd number of its literals are true. An XOR formula is a conjunction of XOR clauses, encoded like a CNF formula with \oplus in place of \lor. Let \mathsf{XOR}\text{-}\mathtt{SAT}= \{\, \langle F \rangle : F \text{ is a satisfiable XOR formula} \,\}. Show that \mathsf{XOR}\text{-}\mathtt{SAT}\in \mathbf{P}.

TipHint

Identify true with 1 and false with 0, and work in the field \mathbb{F}_2 = \{0,1\}, where addition is \oplus. Then \lnot x = 1 + x, so each XOR clause becomes a linear equation in the variables. For example, x_1 \oplus \lnot x_2 \oplus x_3 becomes x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 0. So F is satisfiable if and only if a system of linear equations over \mathbb{F}_2 has a solution. Use Gaussian elimination, and count the number of operations it needs. Why does it matter for the running time that every number that appears is a single bit?

Exercise 33.12 (\mathsf{DNF}\text{-}\mathtt{SAT}) A term is a conjunction of one or more literals. A formula in disjunctive normal form (DNF) is a disjunction of one or more terms, for example (x_1 \land \lnot x_2) \lor (\lnot x_1 \land x_2 \land x_3). DNF formulas are encoded like CNF formulas with the roles of \land and \lor swapped. Let \mathsf{DNF}\text{-}\mathtt{SAT}= \{\, \langle F \rangle : F \text{ is a satisfiable DNF formula} \,\}.

  1. Show that \mathsf{DNF}\text{-}\mathtt{SAT}\in \mathbf{P}.
  2. Every CNF formula is equivalent to a DNF formula (distribute \land over \lor). Explain why this does not show that \mathtt{SAT}\in \mathbf{P}.
  3. Let F_n = (x_1 \lor y_1) \land (x_2 \lor y_2) \land \cdots \land (x_n \lor y_n). Show that every DNF formula equivalent to F_n has at least 2^n terms.
TipHint

For a., a DNF formula is satisfiable if and only if one of its terms is satisfiable. When is a single term satisfiable?

For b., think about how long the translation takes as a function of |\langle F \rangle|.

For c., for each S \subseteq \{1,\dots,n\}, let a_S be the assignment that sets x_i = 1 and y_i = 0 for i \in S, and x_i = 0 and y_i = 1 for i \notin S. Let F' be a DNF formula equivalent to F_n, and let t be a term of F' satisfied by a_S. Show that t must contain x_i for every i \in S and y_i for every i \notin S. (If t is missing, say, x_i, change a_S by setting x_i = 0. Then t is still true but F_n is false.) Conclude that different sets S need different terms.

  1. The values 0 and 1 play the role of the thruth values false and true respectively.↩︎
