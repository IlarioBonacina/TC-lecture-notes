Exercise 22.4 (Matching languages and grammars)  

Consider the languages A = \{a^i b^j c^k :i = j + k\} \qquad B = \{a^i b^j c^k :j = i + k\} \qquad C = \{a^i b^j c^k :k = i + j\} and the context-free grammars G_1 : \left\{\begin{array}{ccl} S\ &\boldsymbol{\to}&\ S_1 S_2 \\ S_1\ &\boldsymbol{\to}&\ a S_1 b \ \vert\ \lambda \\ S_2\ &\boldsymbol{\to}&\ b S_2 c \ \vert\ \lambda \end{array}\right. \qquad G_2 : \left\{\begin{array}{ccl} S\ &\boldsymbol{\to}&\ a S c \ \vert\ S' \\ S'\ &\boldsymbol{\to}&\ b S' c \ \vert\ \lambda \end{array}\right. \qquad G_3 : \left\{\begin{array}{ccl} S\ &\boldsymbol{\to}&\ a S c \ \vert\ S' \\ S'\ &\boldsymbol{\to}&\ a S' b \ \vert\ \lambda \end{array}\right. Which of the following options is correct?

A. A = \mathscr{L}(G_3), B = \mathscr{L}(G_2), C = \mathscr{L}(G_1).

A = \mathscr{L}(G_3) is right, but the other two are swapped. For example, ac \in \mathscr{L}(G_2), using S \Rightarrow aSc \Rightarrow aS'c \Rightarrow ac. But ac \notin B, because B requires j = i + k, that is, 0 = 1 + 1. So B \neq \mathscr{L}(G_2).

B. A = \mathscr{L}(G_3), B = \mathscr{L}(G_1), C = \mathscr{L}(G_2).

Read each grammar from the outside in:

  • In G_3, the rule S\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ aSc produces a^k \cdots c^k, and then S'\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ aS'b fills the middle with a^j b^j. So \mathscr{L}(G_3) = \{a^{j+k} b^j c^k\} = A.
  • In G_1, S_1 produces a^i b^i and S_2 produces b^k c^k. So \mathscr{L}(G_1) = \{a^i b^{i+k} c^k\} = B.
  • In G_2, S\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ aSc produces a^i \cdots c^i, and then S'\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ bS'c fills the middle with b^j c^j. So \mathscr{L}(G_2) = \{a^i b^j c^{i+j}\} = C.
C. A = \mathscr{L}(G_2), B = \mathscr{L}(G_3), C = \mathscr{L}(G_1).

None of the three equalities holds. For example, bc \in \mathscr{L}(G_2), using S \Rightarrow S' \Rightarrow bS'c \Rightarrow bc. But bc \notin A, because A requires i = j + k, that is, 0 = 1 + 1. In G_2, the number of c’s is the sum of the other two counts, so \mathscr{L}(G_2) = C.

D. A = \mathscr{L}(G_1), B = \mathscr{L}(G_2), C = \mathscr{L}(G_3).

None of the three equalities holds. For example, bc \in \mathscr{L}(G_1), using S \Rightarrow S_1 S_2 \Rightarrow S_2 \Rightarrow bS_2c \Rightarrow bc. But bc \notin A, because A requires 0 = 1 + 1. In G_1, the b’s are matched both with the a’s and with the c’s, so the number of b’s is the sum of the other two counts: \mathscr{L}(G_1) = B.

Exercise 22.5 (Same language, different ambiguity)  

Consider the following grammars: G_1 : \left\{\begin{array}{ccl} S\ &\boldsymbol{\to}&\ AB \\ A\ &\boldsymbol{\to}&\ Aaa \ \vert\ a \ \vert\ \lambda \\ B\ &\boldsymbol{\to}&\ Baa \ \vert\ aa \ \vert\ \lambda \end{array}\right. \qquad G_2 : \left\{\begin{array}{ccl} S\ &\boldsymbol{\to}&\ A \ \vert\ B \ \vert\ \lambda \\ A\ &\boldsymbol{\to}&\ Aaa \ \vert\ a \\ B\ &\boldsymbol{\to}&\ Baa \ \vert\ aa \end{array}\right.

A. \mathscr{L}(G_1) \neq \mathscr{L}(G_2) and both are ambiguous.

Both grammars generate a^*. In G_1, A generates every a^n and B generates every a^{2k}, so AB generates all of a^*. In G_2, A generates the words a^n with n odd, B those with n \geqslant 2 even, and S\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ \lambda the empty word. Moreover, G_2 is not ambiguous (see the correct answer).

B. \mathscr{L}(G_1) = \mathscr{L}(G_2) and exactly one is ambiguous.

Both grammars generate a^*, as explained in the other options.

  • G_1 is ambiguous. The word aa has two derivation trees: S \Rightarrow AB \Rightarrow Aaa\,B \Rightarrow aaB \Rightarrow aa, where A produces aa and B produces \lambda; and S \Rightarrow AB \Rightarrow B \Rightarrow aa, where A produces \lambda and B produces aa. (B is ambiguous on its own, since aa can be derived as B \Rightarrow aa, using B\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ aa, or as B \Rightarrow Baa \Rightarrow aa, using B\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ Baa and then B\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ \lambda.)
  • G_2 is unambiguous. A nonempty word a^n is derived from A if n is odd and from B if n is even, never from both. From A, the only derivation of a^n applies A\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ Aaa exactly (n-1)/2 times and then A\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ a. Similarly for B, and \lambda only comes from S\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ \lambda.
C. \mathscr{L}(G_1) \neq \mathscr{L}(G_2) and exactly one is ambiguous.

It is true that exactly one grammar is ambiguous, namely G_1, but the languages are equal. Both are a^*: G_1 combines any number of a’s from A with an even number from B, while G_2 generates the odd words with A, the nonempty even words with B, and \lambda directly.

D. \mathscr{L}(G_1) = \mathscr{L}(G_2) and both are ambiguous.

The languages are equal, both a^*, but G_2 is not ambiguous. Its three alternatives A, B and \lambda generate disjoint sets of words (odd lengths, nonempty even lengths, and the empty word), and inside A and B each word has a single derivation.

Exercise 22.6 (Cleaning up a grammar)  

Consider the following grammar, which has 5 productions: \begin{array}{ccl} S\ &\boldsymbol{\to}&\ A\,B \\ A\ &\boldsymbol{\to}&\ a\,A \ \vert\ \lambda \\ B\ &\boldsymbol{\to}&\ b\,B \ \vert\ \lambda \end{array} How many productions does the grammar have after cleaning it up? In the cleaned-up grammar, you do not need to introduce a new initial symbol, but you do need to remove the \lambda-productions and the unit productions.

A. 12

This is too many. You probably kept the unit productions S\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ A and S\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ B in addition to the productions that replace them. The cleaned-up grammar has 10 productions (see the correct answer).

B. 11

This is one too many. Check that no unit production (S\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ A or S\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ B) and no \lambda-production other than S\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ \lambda is left. The cleaned-up grammar has 10 productions (see the correct answer).

C. 10

Removing \lambda-productions. A, B and S can all derive \lambda. For every production, we add the versions with the nullable symbols deleted, and then remove A\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ \lambda and B\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ \lambda. Since \lambda \in \mathscr{L}(G) and S does not appear on any right-hand side, we keep S\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ \lambda: S\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ AB \ \vert\ A \ \vert\ B \ \vert\ \lambda \qquad A\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ aA \ \vert\ a \qquad B\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ bB \ \vert\ b. Removing unit productions. We replace S\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ A and S\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ B by the productions of A and B: S\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ AB \ \vert\ aA \ \vert\ a \ \vert\ bB \ \vert\ b \ \vert\ \lambda \qquad A\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ aA \ \vert\ a \qquad B\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ bB \ \vert\ b. There are no useless symbols, so the result has 6 + 2 + 2 = 10 productions.

D. 13

This is too many. Removing \lambda-productions and unit productions makes some productions disappear, so check that A\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ \lambda, B\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ \lambda, S\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ A and S\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ B are no longer there. The cleaned-up grammar has 10 productions (see the correct answer).

Exercise 22.7 (Choosing a word for the pumping lemma)  

We want to show that L = \{a^m b^n c^m d^n :m, n \geqslant 0\} is not context-free by applying the pumping lemma for context-free languages directly. Given the pumping length N, we want to choose a word w as in the argument that applies the contrapositive of the lemma. Which of the following choices has any chance of completing the argument correctly?

A. w = a^N b^N c^N d^N.

This choice works. Write w = uvxyz with |vxy| \leqslant N and |vy| \geqslant 1. Since each block has length N, the part vxy meets at most two adjacent blocks: a and b, b and c, or c and d. Pumping changes the number of letters in those blocks only. But the matched pairs are a with c and b with d, which are never adjacent, so pumping changes one block of a pair without the other. Hence uv^2xy^2z \notin L. (If v or y contains two different letters, pumping also breaks the order of the letters.)

B. w = a b^N c d^N.

This choice cannot work, because some decompositions can be pumped. Take v = b, x = c and y = d, using the last b, the c and the first d. Then |vxy| = 3 \leqslant N, and uv^ixy^iz = a\, b^{N-1+i}\, c\, d^{N-1+i} \in L \quad \text{for every } i \geqslant 0, because the numbers of b’s and d’s grow together. The single c in the middle lets vxy reach both blocks that must match.

C. w = a^N b c^N d.

This choice cannot work, for the same reason as the previous one. Take v = a, x = b and y = c, using the last a, the b and the first c. Then |vxy| = 3 \leqslant N, and uv^ixy^iz = a^{N-1+i}\, b\, c^{N-1+i}\, d \in L \quad \text{for every } i \geqslant 0. Since the b block is short, vxy can reach both the a’s and the c’s and pump them together.

D. w = abcd.

The pumping lemma only says something about words of length at least N. Since N is given to us and may be larger than 4, we cannot use w = abcd. The word must depend on N.

Exercise 22.8 (A pushdown automaton for a^n b^n c^n?)  

We want to recognize the language L = \{a^n b^n c^n :n \geqslant 0\} with the following PDA P. Given a word, P

  1. first pushes AA for each a;
  2. then pops one A for each b;
  3. finally pops one A for each c;
  4. and accepts if the stack is empty when the input ends.

Which option is correct?

A. \mathscr{L}(P) = L.

P only checks that the total number of b’s and c’s is twice the number of a’s: it accepts a^n b^j c^k exactly when j + k = 2n. For example, abb is accepted, since it pushes AA and then pops two A’s, but abb \notin L.

B. \mathscr{L}(P) \neq L, but another PDA could recognize it.

It is true that \mathscr{L}(P) \neq L, since for example abb \in \mathscr{L}(P) \setminus L. But no PDA can recognize L: the languages accepted by PDAs are exactly the context-free languages, and L = \{a^n b^n c^n\} is not context-free, as can be shown with the pumping lemma for context-free languages.

C. \mathscr{L}(P) \supsetneq L and no other PDA recognizes it.

P accepts exactly the words a^n b^j c^k with j + k = 2n. Every word a^n b^n c^n satisfies this condition, so L \subseteq \mathscr{L}(P). The inclusion is strict, because abb \in \mathscr{L}(P) \setminus L. Moreover, L is not context-free, by the pumping lemma, so no PDA recognizes it. A single stack can compare the a’s with the b’s, or with the c’s, but not with both.

D. \mathscr{L}(P) \subsetneq L and no other PDA recognizes it.

The second part is right, but the inclusion goes the other way. Every word of L is accepted: on a^n b^n c^n, P pushes 2n symbols and pops n + n = 2n. So L \subseteq \mathscr{L}(P), and P accepts more words than it should, for example abb.

Exercise 22.9 (Even binary values (Partial exam 2, 05/06/2026))  

Consider the language L = \{\, w \in \{\textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{0}},\textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{1}}\}^* :\mathrm{value}_2(w) \in 2\mathbb{N} \,\} \setminus \{\, \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{1}}^n \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{0}}^n :n \geqslant 0 \,\}. What can we say about L?

Note that the set difference is defined as A \setminus B = A \cap \overline{B} for any sets A and B.

A. \overline{L} is a language accepted by a pushdown automaton, but L is not.

L is also accepted by a pushdown automaton. Let E = \{w :\mathrm{value}_2(w) \in 2\mathbb{N}\} and D = \{\textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{1}}^n \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{0}}^n :n \geqslant 0\}, so that L = E \cap \overline{D}. The language E is regular: it consists of the empty word and the words ending in \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{0}}. The complement \overline{D} is context-free. The intersection of a context-free language with a regular language is context-free, so L is context-free.

B. Neither L nor \overline{L} are languages accepted by pushdown automata.

Both are. The languages accepted by pushdown automata are exactly the context-free languages. With E and D as in the other options, L = E \cap \overline{D} is the intersection of a regular and a context-free language, and \overline{L} = \overline{E} \cup D is the union of a regular and a context-free language. Both operations preserve context-freeness.

C. L is a language accepted by a pushdown automaton, but \overline{L} is not.

It is true that context-free languages are not closed under complement in general, but here \overline{L} is context-free too. With E and D as in the other options, De Morgan’s laws give \overline{L} = \overline{E \cap \overline{D}} = \overline{E} \cup D. \overline{E} is regular (the words ending in \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{1}}), D = \{\textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{1}}^n \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{0}}^n\} is context-free, and the union of two context-free languages is context-free.

D. Both L and \overline{L} are languages accepted by pushdown automata.

The languages accepted by pushdown automata are exactly the context-free languages. Let E = \{w :\mathrm{value}_2(w) \in 2\mathbb{N}\}, which is regular (the empty word and the words ending in \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{0}}), and D = \{\textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{1}}^n \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{0}}^n :n \geqslant 0\}.

  • Both D and \overline{D} are context-free. For \overline{D}: a word is not in D if and only if it is not of the form \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{1}}^i \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{0}}^j, which is a regular condition, or it is of the form \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{1}}^i \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{0}}^j with i \neq j, which is context-free.
  • L = E \cap \overline{D} is the intersection of a context-free language with a regular one, hence context-free.
  • \overline{L} = \overline{E} \cup D is the union of a regular language and a context-free one, hence context-free.
