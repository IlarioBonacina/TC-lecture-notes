17  Parsing and Ambiguity

In this chapter we expand on the concept of parsing and ambiguity. They come into play since one important use of context-free grammars is to specify all the valid programs of a given programming language.

Definition 17.1 Given a context-free grammar G=(V,\Sigma,R,S) and a word w\in \mathscr{L}(G), a parse tree for w is a rooted tree with internal vertices labeled with symbols in V and leaves with symbols in \Sigma\cup\{\varepsilon\}:

  • the root has label S
  • if a vertex has label A and its children read from left to right form the word \alpha\in (\Sigma\cup V)^* then A\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ \alpha\in R;
  • the leaves (again read from left to right) form the word w (the leaves with label \varepsilon contribute nothing).

Now, to each derivation of w we can associate a parse tree (using the rules of the derivation) and viceversa, a parse tree may correspond to several possible derivations of w, depending on the order in which the non-terminals are expanded.

If the non-terminal variables describe operations, then we can use a parse tree to associate a priority among the operations and hence an intended meaning of the word w.

Example 17.1 Let’s construct a grammar generating all valid numeric expressions using sums \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{+}} and differences \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{-}} (for sake of simplicity without parentheses):

\begin{array}{lcl} E\ &\boldsymbol{\to}&\ E \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{+}} E\ \vert\ E \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{-}} E\ \vert\ N\\ N\ &\boldsymbol{\to}&\ D\ \vert\ D'N\\ D'\ &\boldsymbol{\to}&\ \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{1}}\ \vert\ \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{2}}\ \vert\ \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{3}}\ \vert\ \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{4}}\ \vert\ \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{5}}\ \vert\ \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{6}}\ \vert\ \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{7}}\ \vert\ \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{8}}\ \vert\ \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{9}}\\ D\ &\boldsymbol{\to}&\ \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{0}}\ \vert\ D' \end{array}

or in a more readable syntax

If we want to use the grammar above not just to generate words but to give a meaning to them we have a problem! Do we need to do first the difference (as in Figure 17.1) or the sum as in Figure 17.2? This problem is called ambiguity.

Definition 17.2 A grammar G is ambiguous if there exist a word w\in \mathscr{L}(G) with two distinct ordered, labeled parse trees for w.

Exercise 17.1 (🌱) Show that the grammar for the \mathsf{Dyck}\text{-}1 language we saw in Equation 16.1 is ambiguous. (For example giving two non-isomorphic parse trees for \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{()()()}})

A common way to check that two parse trees for a word w are non-isomorphic is to do a pre-order (Root, Left, Right) traversal on both of them. This corresponds to the so called leftmost derivation of w: at each step of the derivation of w the non-terminal to expand is the leftmost in the string. Two leftmost derivations are distinct if at some step either they expand a different non-terminal or they expand the same non-terminal but with a different rule. Using a post-order traversal gives the notion of rightmost derivation.

Example 17.2 One famous example of ambiguos grammar is given by the grammar for the programming language C. A simplified grammar exhibiting the dangling else ambiguity looks as follows:

\begin{array}{lcl} \langle\mathbf{Statement}\rangle\ &\boldsymbol{\to}&\ \dots\ \vert\ \langle\mathbf{Selection\text{-}statement}\rangle\\ \langle\mathbf{Selection\text{-}statement}\rangle\ &\boldsymbol{\to}&\ \dots\ \vert\ \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{if(}} \langle Expression\rangle\textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{)}}\ \langle\mathbf{Statement}\rangle \ \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{else}}\ \langle\mathbf{Statement}\rangle\\ \langle\mathbf{Selection\text{-}statement}\rangle\ &\boldsymbol{\to}&\ \dots\ \vert\ \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{if(}} \langle Expression\rangle\textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{)}}\ \langle\mathbf{Statement}\rangle \end{array} This gives rise to the famous Dangling else problem which is due to the above grammar being ambiguous. How do we interpret the following string?

\textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{if(a)\ if(b)\ s1;\ else\ s2;}} can be parsed either as

if(a) {
    if(b)
        s1;
    else
        s2;
}

or as

if (a) {
    if (b)
        s1;
}
else
    s2;

The C standard imposes to interpret a possibly ambiguous if-then-else as the first tree, but this is not imposed by the grammar itself.

Sometimes it is possible to remove the ambibuity from a grammar giving another grammar generating the same language but now unambiguous. Other times it is not possible, there are languages in \mathbf{CFL} where every possible grammar generating them is ambiguous (they are called inherently ambiguous context-free languages).

This is a difficult problem! There is no algorithm to decide whether a given context-free grammar is ambiguous!

17.1 The CYK algorithm

Given a grammar G=(V,\Sigma, R,S) and a word w a natural problem is to decide whether w\in \mathscr{L}(G) and if this is the case to construct a parse tree for w. In this section, we describe an algorithm to solve this problem: the Cocke–Younger–Kasami (CYK) parsing algorithm. This is an example of the common technique of dynamic programming.

Important

A reference for the CYK algorithm is (Kozen 1997, chap. 27).

TipAssumption on the form of G

For simplicity, we assume all the productions of G have the form A\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ BC with A,B,C\in V or A\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{s}} with \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{s}}\in \Sigma (or S\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ \varepsilon if \varepsilon\in \mathscr{L}(G)).

As we see in Chapter 18 this is not an unreasonable assumption since every context-free grammar can be transformed into an equivalent one of this form and the new grammar has size at most |G|^2, where |G| denotes the size of the grammar (for example, the total length of its productions).

Exercise 17.7 Show that for a grammar G of the form above any production of a non-empty word of length n consists of the application of exactly 2n-1 productions.

By the special form of the grammar, if w=\varepsilon it is trivial to check whether w\in \mathscr{L}(G). So we can also assume w\neq \varepsilon. Let n=|w|.

The idea of the algorithm is not only to decide whether w\in \mathscr{L}(G), but for each subword of w to know what variables can generate it. To store this information we can use Boolean variables P[i,\ell,A], for 1\leq i\leq n, 1\leq \ell\leq n-i+1 and A\in V, with intended meaning the truth value of “the subword of w starting in position i and of length \ell can be generated from the variable A”. (As usual, we use the convention that w[1] is the first symbol of w, that is w=w[1]w[2]\cdots w[n].)

Our goal is to compute the truth value of P[1,n,S] since P[1,n,S]=1 if and only if w\in \mathscr{L}(G) by construction.

It is immediate to know the values of P[i,1,A] for each i\leq n and A\in V. How?

NoteAnswer

P[i,1,A]=1 if and only if A\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ w[i] is a production rule of G. Notice that here we are using crucially the assumption on the special form of the grammar G.

Now, in a typical dynamic programming approach, you want to compute all truth values P[i,\ell,A] assuming you already know all the truth values P[i,k,A] with k<\ell. How

NoteAnswer

For a fixed i with 1\leq i\leq n, P[i,\ell,A]=1 if and only if exists 1\leq k\leq \ell-1 and a production A\ \boldsymbol{\to}\ BC, where both P[i,k,B] and P[i+k,\ell-k,C] are both true.

Exercise 17.8 Using the CYK algorithm as described above, decide whether w=\textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{aabbab}} is in \mathscr{L}(G) where G is the following grammar

\begin{array}{lcl} S\ &\boldsymbol{\to}&\ AB\ \vert\ BA \ \vert\ SS\ \vert\ AC\ \vert\ BD\\ C\ &\boldsymbol{\to}&\ SB\\ D\ &\boldsymbol{\to}&\ SA\\ A\ &\boldsymbol{\to}&\ \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{a}}\\ B\ &\boldsymbol{\to}&\ \textcolor{darkred}{\mathtt{b}} \end{array}

Following this approach, a possible pseudocode implementing the CYK algorithm is the following.

\begin{algorithm} \caption{CYK Algorithm}\begin{algorithmic} \Input A non-empty word $w = w[1] \cdots w[n]$ and a grammar $G=(V,\Sigma,R,S)$ meeting the assumptions above \Output The answer to the question: $w\in \mathscr{L}(G)$? \Comment{Assume all $P[i,\ell,A]$ are initialized to $0$} \For{$i = 1$ to $n$} \Comment{Initialization: setting all $P[i,1,A]$s} \ForAll{$A \in V$} \If{$(A \to w[i]) \in R$} \State $P[i,1,A] = 1$ \EndIf \EndFor \EndFor \For{$\ell = 2$ to $n$} \Comment{Fill the table in increasing order of substring length} \For{$i = 1$ to $n-\ell+1$} \For{$k = 1$ to $\ell-1$} \ForAll{$(A \to BC) \in R$} \If{$P[i,k,B] \land P[i+k,\ell-k,C]$} \State $P[i,\ell,A] = 1$ \EndIf \EndFor \EndFor \EndFor \EndFor \State \Return $P[1,n,S]$ \end{algorithmic} \end{algorithm}

The cost of the previous algorithm is \mathcal{O}(n^3|G|).

A natural question to ask is whether the CYK algorithm we described has the optimal running time \mathcal{O}(n^3|G|) for deciding whether w\in \mathscr{L}(G). This is not the case and (surprisingly) parsing for context-free grammars is tightly connected with matrix multiplication of 0/1-valued matrices. On one side Valiant (1975) showed that algorithms for fast multiplication of 0/1-valued matrices can be used to do the computations in the CYK algorithm. On the other side Lee (2002) showed that any parsing algorithm for CFGs with running time \mathcal{O}(n^{3-\varepsilon}|G|) can be turned into an algorithm to compute the product of n\times n 0/1-valued matrices in time \mathcal{O}(n^{3-\varepsilon/3}).

Kozen, Dexter C. 1997. Automata and Computability. Springer New York. https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4612-1844-9.
Lee, Lillian. 2002. “Fast Context-Free Grammar Parsing Requires Fast Boolean Matrix Multiplication.” Journal of the ACM 49 (1): 1–15. https://doi.org/10.1145/505241.505242.
Valiant, Leslie G. 1975. “General Context-Free Recognition in Less Than Cubic Time.” Journal of Computer and System Sciences 10 (2): 308–15. https://doi.org/10.1016/s0022-0000(75)80046-8.
